Physical / Mechanical Weathering

No Chemical Change

Disintegration of rocks into smaller fragments without altering their internal mineral composition.

a) Freeze-Thaw / Frost Shattering

Animated: Freeze Expansion & Rock Displacement

Water enters rock joints, freezes at night, and expands by approximately 9%. Repeated freeze-thaw cycles exert tremendous outward pressure, widening cracks until fragments shatter off.

b) Thermal Expansion / Exfoliation

Animated: Diurnal Heating & Concentric Peeling

Prevalent in hot deserts. Extreme diurnal temperature ranges cause outer rock layers to expand during daytime heating and contract at night, causing outer shells to peel like onion skins.

c) Pressure Release / Unloading

Removal of heavy overlying rock masses by erosion reduces confining pressure on underlying igneous rocks, allowing them to expand upward and fracture parallel to the surface.

d) Salt Crystallization

Saline solutions enter pores in arid or coastal rocks. As water evaporates, growing salt crystals exert internal pressure against surrounding mineral grains, causing granular disintegration.

e) Abrasion & Attrition

Abrasion: Rock particles carried by wind, water, or ice scour and wear away exposed rock surfaces like sandpaper.

Attrition: Transported rock fragments smash against one another, breaking into progressively smaller, smoother, and rounder pieces.

Chemical Weathering

Alters Mineral Composition

Decomposition of rocks through chemical reactions that alter their mineral structure. Most active in hot, humid climates.

a) Carbonation

CO2 + H2O → H2CO3 (Carbonic Acid)
CaCO3 + H2CO3 → Ca(HCO3)2 (Soluble)

Rainwater absorbs atmospheric CO2 to form weak carbonic acid. This reacts with insoluble calcium carbonate in limestone and chalk, converting it into soluble calcium bicarbonate.

b) Oxidation

Animated: Iron oxidation to ferric oxide (Rusting)
4Fe2+ + 3O2 + 2H2O → 2Fe2O3·H2O (Rust)

Oxygen dissolves in water and reacts with iron-rich minerals (e.g., olivine, pyroxene). The iron oxidizes from ferrous to ferric forms, creating reddish-brown rust that weakens the rock framework.

c) Hydrolysis

Feldspar + H2O + CO2 → Kaolinite (Clay) + Silicic Acid

Hydrogen ions (H+) or hydroxyl ions (OH-) in water react directly with rock minerals. For example, hard feldspar in granite reacts with water to form soft kaolin clay, softening the rock matrix.

d) Hydration

Fe2O3 (Hematite) + 3H2O → Fe2O3·3H2O (Limonite)

Minerals absorb water directly into their crystalline lattice, expanding their structure. This expansion creates internal stress and causes the rock to crumble.

e) Solution & Chelation

Solution: Highly soluble minerals (e.g., rock salt / halite) dissolve completely in pure water without requiring acid.

Chelation: Organic acids secreted by living lichens and decaying organic humus bind to metallic ions, dissolving minerals out of the rock structure.

Biological Weathering

Physical + Chemical Combined

Disintegration and decomposition of rocks driven by living organisms (plants, animals, microorganisms, and humans).

a) Plant Root Wedging (Physical)

Seeds fall into rock fissures. As plants germinate, roots penetrate deep into cracks searching for moisture. As roots thicken, they exert immense mechanical pressure that splits the rock apart. Local Example: Fig trees splitting walls in Harare CBD.

b) Lichens, Mosses & Animals (Chemical & Physical)

Lichens & Mosses: Pioneer species that release humic and lichenic acids directly onto rock surfaces, dissolving mineral grains while retaining moisture for ongoing chemical attack.

Burrowing Animals: Termites, earthworms, and rodents break up rock fragments and bring unweathered minerals to the surface.

Sample Exam Questions & Answering Strategies

ZIMSEC 3-Mark Rule for "Describe Biological Weathering":
Always include ONE physical mechanism (e.g., tree root wedging) AND ONE chemical mechanism (e.g., organic lichen acids) along with explicit detail to secure full marks.

Key Roles of Weathering:

  1. Prepares Rock for Erosion: Weakens and fractures bedrock so agents like water and wind can transport debris.
  2. Soil Formation: Combines weathered parent rock material with decayed organic matter (humus) to form fertile soil.
